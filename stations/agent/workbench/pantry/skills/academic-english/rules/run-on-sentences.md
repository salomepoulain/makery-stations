# Run-on sentences

A run-on joins two or more independent clauses without the connector needed to hold them together. Three shapes show up most:

1. **Fused sentence**: independent clauses sit back to back with no mark between them at all.
   - *The server crashed twice overnight the logs showed no clear cause.*
2. **Comma splice**: a comma alone tries to do the job of a real joint.
   - *The results looked promising, the sample size was too small to trust them.*
3. **Conjunction pileup**: three or more clauses chained with and/but/so, so the sentence keeps going past the point where the reader can hold it all.
   - *The team rewrote the parser and it passed the old tests and then a new edge case broke it and nobody had time to fix it before the release.*

## Fixes

- **Split with a period.** Give each clause its own sentence when the ideas don't need to stay this close. *The server crashed twice overnight. The logs showed no clear cause.*
- **Join with a semicolon.** Use this when the two clauses are close enough in meaning that separating them into full sentences would lose that link. *The results looked promising; the sample size was too small to trust them.*
- **Add a coordinating conjunction after the comma.** A comma alone can't join two independent clauses, but comma + and/but/or/so/yet/nor/for can. *The team rewrote the parser, and it passed the old tests.*
- **Subordinate one clause.** Turn the less important clause into a dependent one with a word like because, although, or when, so it leans on the other instead of standing next to it. *The results looked promising, although the sample size was too small to trust them.*

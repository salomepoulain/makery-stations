# Word choice

Pick the word that names the thing, not the word that's lying around.

## Reach for the specific word

Vague filler ("stuff," "things," "interesting," "good," "bad") forces the reader to guess what you mean. If no precise word comes to mind, the problem usually sits one level down: the idea itself is still fuzzy. Sharpen the idea and the word follows.

- Weak: "The results showed some interesting stuff about user behavior."
- Better: "The results showed that users abandoned the checkout flow after the second form field."

## Prefer a single strong verb to a verb-plus-preposition combination

Academic prose reads cleaner with one-word verbs than with phrasal verbs built from a common verb and a preposition.

- "generate" not "come up with"
- "investigate" not "look into"
- "resolve" not "sort out"
- "compile" not "pull together"

## Cut clichés

Stock phrases ("at the end of the day," "few and far between," "the elephant in the room") have been repeated so often that readers skim past them without absorbing anything. They also usually spend more words than the idea needs. State the point in your own words instead.

- Cliché: "At the end of the day, the sample size was too small to draw firm conclusions."
- Direct: "The sample size was too small to draw firm conclusions."

## Skip archaic or ornate vocabulary

Words like "heretofore," "whilst," "amongst," and "rendered asunder" signal that the writer is reaching for a register no one actually speaks in anymore. They read as affected rather than formal.

## Write for the reader, not for effect

Academic writing exists to make an argument land, not to prove the writer owns a thesaurus. Skip jargon that isn't doing real work. When a technical term is the only accurate word for the concept, use it, but use it because it's correct, not because it sounds impressive.

## Mistakes to watch for

- **Wrong word entirely**: writing "allude" when you mean "elude," or "affect" when you mean "effect."
- **Wrong shade of meaning**: two words can share a dictionary definition and still carry different implications; "notorious" and "famous" both mean "widely known," but only one of them is a compliment.
- **Ambiguous pronouns**: if a reader has to reread the sentence to figure out what "this" or "they" points back to, replace the pronoun with the actual noun.
- **Jargon for show**: inserting a technical term where a plain word would say the same thing just as accurately.

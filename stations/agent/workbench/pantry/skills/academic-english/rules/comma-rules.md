# Comma rules

1. **Joining two independent clauses.** When a coordinating conjunction (and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet) links two clauses that could each stand alone as a sentence, put a comma before the conjunction.
   Example: The server restarted cleanly, but the cache never repopulated.

2. **After an opening clause, phrase, or word.** A comma follows any introductory material, whether it's a full clause, a shorter phrase, or a single word, before the main clause begins.
   Example: After the migration finished, the team ran the full test suite.

3. **Bracketing nonessential material.** When a clause, phrase, or word in the middle of a sentence could be removed without changing the sentence's core meaning, set it off with a comma on each side, one to mark where the aside starts and one to mark where it ends.
   Example: The intern, who had joined only that week, spotted the bug first.

4. **Leaving essential material unmarked.** Don't add commas around a clause the sentence actually depends on for its meaning, including most "that" clauses. A "that" clause attached to a noun is always essential, and so is a "that" clause following a verb of mental action (think, believe, know, and similar verbs).
   Example: The rule that no comma precedes this clause is easy to forget.

5. **Items in a series.** When three or more words, phrases, or clauses are listed together, separate each one with a comma.
   Example: The dataset included timestamps, wallet addresses, and transaction amounts.

6. **Stacked adjectives before a noun.** When two or more adjectives each independently modify the same noun, separate them with commas, but don't place a comma between the last adjective and the noun, and don't add commas between adjectives that aren't independent of each other (where one adjective actually modifies the phrase formed by the next adjective plus the noun).
   Example: A tall, weathered lighthouse stood at the edge of the cliff.

7. **Geographic names, dates, addresses, and titles.** Surround these with commas: place names given as a sequence, the year in a full date (but not the month-day pair), the parts of a street address (but not the house number and street name), and a title that follows a person's name.
   Example: The shipment left on March 4, 2019, from 88 Harbor Street, Newport, Rhode Island, addressed to Maria Chen, logistics lead at the port authority.

# Style

Good academic style rests on two things: clarity and texture.

- **Clarity** means a reader gets one meaning from your sentence, not two or three. Nothing about style should stand between the reader and the idea.
- **Texture** is the voice underneath the clarity: the rhythm of your sentences, the words you reach for, the sense that a specific person wrote this for a specific audience.

## Checklist for revising a draft

1. **Cut the dead weight.** Strip out stock phrases, needless passive voice, clichés, and any sentence that says twice what it could say once.
   - Weak: "It should be noted that the results of the experiment were found to be consistent with what was expected." Strong: "The results matched our predictions."
2. **Only use a word if you could explain it to someone else.** Reaching for a fancier word than you actually command backfires; a misused big word costs more credibility than a plain one ever would.
   - Weak: "The findings evince a marked deleterious effect on yield." Strong: "The findings show yield dropped sharply."
3. **Mix short and long sentences.** A paragraph of uniform sentence length, whether all clipped or all sprawling, wears the reader down before the argument does.
   - Flat: "We ran the trial. We measured output. We recorded the results. We compared them to baseline." Better: "We ran the trial and measured output, then compared the results to baseline."
4. **Say it out loud before you call it done.** If your tongue trips on a sentence, a reader's eye will trip on it too, so rewrite anything that doesn't come out smoothly when spoken.
   - Trips: "The methodology employed herein, insofar as it departs from prior approaches, warrants brief elaboration." Reads clean: "This method differs from prior approaches in one way worth explaining."
5. **Ideas come before polish.** No amount of graceful phrasing rescues an argument with nothing behind it; get the thinking right first, then dress it well.

# General rules of style for humanities writing

## Quoting dialogue from a play, film, or show
When a quotation runs longer than four lines of dialogue, set it off as a block instead of running it into your paragraph. Capitalize each speaker's name, indent it one inch from the margin, and follow it with a period before the line begins. If a single speaker's line wraps to a second line, indent that continuation an extra quarter inch (about three spaces) so it reads as part of the same speech, not a new speaker.

- WILLY. I don't say he's a great man. Willy Loman never made a lot of money. His name was never in the paper. He's not the finest character that ever lived. But he's a human being, and a terrible thing is happening to him.
- BIFF. Pop, I'm a dime a dozen, and so are you.

## Integrating a quotation with an in-text citation
You can fold a quotation into your prose in three ways. Pick the one that fits the sentence you're already writing rather than defaulting to the same pattern every time.

- After an attribution clause, set off with a comma: Reyes writes, "The archive remembers what the city chose to forget" (Reyes 118).
- After a full sentence that introduces the quotation, set off with a colon: Reyes frames the demolition as an act of erasure rather than renewal: "The archive remembers what the city chose to forget" (Reyes 118).
- Woven directly into your own sentence, with no punctuation before the quoted words: Reyes argues that the demolished archive "remembers what the city chose to forget" (Reyes 118).

Match the grammar of the quoted words to the sentence around them. If the woven-in version requires you to change a verb tense or pronoun to fit, use brackets around the change rather than silently altering the source.

## Italics versus quotation marks for titles
Treat a title as part of a larger whole or as the whole itself, and format it accordingly.

- Italicize the names of newspapers and journals: *The Boston Globe*, *The Journal of Modern History*.
- Put article, essay, and chapter titles in quotation marks, since they're pieces of a larger publication: "Memory and the Ruined Archive."
- Italicize the titles of films, books, and other stand-alone works: *Children of Men*.

The underlying test isn't length but whether the piece stands alone (italics) or belongs inside a larger container (quotation marks). A novel is italicized; a short story printed inside a collection goes in quotes even if the collection's title is italicized.

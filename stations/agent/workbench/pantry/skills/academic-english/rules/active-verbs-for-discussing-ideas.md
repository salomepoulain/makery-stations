# Active verbs for discussing ideas

When you summarize what a source does, reach for a verb that names the specific move it makes rather than a flat placeholder like "says," "writes about," "believes," or "states." A precise verb tells the reader what kind of intellectual work is happening; a flat one just marks that words were produced.

Bad: Chen says that the model overfits on small samples.
Good: Chen demonstrates that the model overfits on small samples.

Bad: Patel writes about the risks of relying on a single data source.
Good: Patel warns against relying on a single data source.

Bad: The author believes the two frameworks can be combined.
Good: The author proposes combining the two frameworks.

Two words deserve special caution.

"Mentions" implies a passing, undetailed reference. Use it only when a source genuinely brings something up in passing rather than developing it. If the source actually builds an argument around the point, "mentions" undersells it.

Bad: Ortiz mentions that latency scales with model depth, building the rest of her argument on that relationship.
Good: Ortiz shows that latency scales with model depth, building the rest of her argument on that relationship.

"Notion" carries a whiff of the offhand or unserious, as if the idea were a passing fancy rather than a reasoned position. Prefer "idea" or "argument" when a source is making a real claim.

Bad: The notion that markets adjust instantly to new information drives the entire paper.
Good: The idea that markets adjust instantly to new information drives the entire paper.

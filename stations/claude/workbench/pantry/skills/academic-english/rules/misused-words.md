# Misused words

These word pairs get swapped constantly in academic prose because they look or sound alike. Each entry gives the correct sense and a pair of sentences showing the right and wrong use.

1. **adverse / averse.** Adverse describes something harmful or unfavorable; averse describes a person's reluctance toward something. "The drug produced adverse side effects in a third of subjects." Not: "Subjects were adverse to the treatment" (should be "averse to").

2. **appraise / apprise.** Appraise means to judge the value of something; apprise means to inform someone. "The committee will appraise the proposal's feasibility." Not: "We appraised her of the delay" (should be "apprised").

3. **as far as.** This phrase needs a verb like "is concerned" to complete the clause. "As far as funding is concerned, the grant covers two years." Not: "As far as funding, the grant covers two years."

4. **begs the question.** This means an argument assumes the very point it is trying to prove, not that it invites a follow-up question. "Claiming the policy works because it is effective begs the question." Not: "The report begs the question of what happens next" (use "raises the question").

5. **cliché.** This is a noun naming an overused expression, not an adjective describing something as overused. "The introduction opens with a cliché." Not: "The opening is cliché" (use "clichéd").

6. **credible / credulous.** Credible describes a claim or source worth believing; credulous describes a person too willing to believe things. "The witness gave a credible account of the incident." Not: "He was credible enough to accept the scam" (should be "credulous").

7. **criteria / criterion.** Criteria is plural; criterion is singular. "Three criteria determined eligibility." Not: "One criteria stood out" (should be "criterion").

8. **data.** Data is a plural count noun and takes a plural verb in formal writing. "The data show a consistent decline." Not: "The data shows a consistent decline" in strict academic register.

9. **dichotomy.** A dichotomy is a division into two mutually exclusive categories, not just any contrast or gap. "The study frames a dichotomy between treated and untreated groups." Not: "There is a dichotomy in reviewer opinions" when opinions vary along a spectrum (use "difference" or "discrepancy").

10. **discreet / discrete.** Discreet means tactful or careful about what one reveals; discrete means separate or distinct. "She raised the concern discreetly during a private meeting." Not: "The variables are discreetly measured" (should be "discretely," meaning as separate units).

11. **disinterested / uninterested.** Disinterested means impartial; uninterested means bored or lacking interest. "The arbitrator was disinterested and ruled without bias." Not: "The judge seemed disinterested in the case" when the intended meaning is that the judge was bored (should be "uninterested").

12. **enormity / enormousness.** Enormity refers to extreme wickedness; enormousness refers to great size. "Historians still grapple with the enormity of the massacre." Not: "The enormity of the dataset slowed the analysis" (should be "enormousness" or simply "size").

13. **flaunt / flout.** Flaunt means to show off; flout means to openly disregard a rule. "The firm flaunted its new headquarters in the annual report." Not: "The firm flaunted safety regulations" (should be "flouted").

14. **fortuitous.** Fortuitous means happening by chance, not merely lucky. "Their meeting at the conference was entirely fortuitous." Not: "It was fortuitous that the grant renewed on schedule" if the renewal was planned rather than accidental (use "fortunate").

15. **hone / home in.** Hone means to sharpen a skill; home in on means to move steadily toward a target. "She honed her statistical skills over two years." Not: "The analysis honed in on the outlier" (should be "homed in on").

16. **ironic.** Ironic describes an outcome that is strikingly at odds with what was expected, not simply bad timing. "It is ironic that the fire station burned down." Not: "It was ironic that the meeting got rescheduled the day I was free" (that is just inconvenient, not ironic).

17. **irregardless.** This is not a standard word. Use "regardless" or "irrespective." "Regardless of funding, the trial will proceed." Not: "Irregardless of funding, the trial will proceed."

18. **literally.** Literally means something happened exactly as described, not as an intensifier for a figure of speech. "The sample literally froze solid at negative twenty degrees." Not: "The results literally exploded off the chart" unless the chart actually caught fire.

19. **mitigate / militate.** Mitigate means to lessen the severity of something; militate means to serve as a strong reason against something. "The new protocol mitigated the risk of contamination." Not: "These findings mitigate against the hypothesis" (should be "militate against").

20. **noisome.** Noisome means foul-smelling, not loud. "The compound released a noisome gas during the reaction." Not: "The noisome construction outside disrupted the lecture" (use "noisy").

21. **opportunism.** Opportunism means seizing existing opportunities as they appear, not manufacturing new ones. "Her opportunism let her secure funding the moment the call opened." Not: "His opportunism built an entirely new market from nothing," which describes creating rather than seizing a chance.

22. **parameter.** A parameter is a variable that defines a system, not a boundary or limit. "The model's parameters were estimated from training data." Not: "Stay within the parameters of the assignment" (use "limits" or "bounds").

23. **phenomena / phenomenon.** Phenomena is plural; phenomenon is singular. "Several phenomena remain unexplained." Not: "This phenomena remains unexplained" (should be "phenomenon").

24. **practicable / practical.** Practicable means capable of being carried out; practical means sensible or useful in application. "A four-week timeline is not practicable given staffing." Not: "The method is practicable for teaching beginners" when the point is that it works well in practice (use "practical").

25. **protagonist.** A protagonist is the central character driving a narrative, not simply a supporter of a cause. "The protagonist confronts her mentor in the final chapter." Not: "He was a protagonist of the reform bill" (use "proponent").

26. **refute.** Refute means to prove something false with evidence, not merely to deny or dispute it. "The experiment refuted the earlier claim by replicating it under controlled conditions." Not: "The senator refuted the allegation" when he simply denied it without disproving it (use "denied" or "disputed").

27. **simplistic.** Simplistic means oversimplified in a way that misses real complexity, not simply plain or elegant. "The model offers a simplistic account of a multi-causal problem." Not: "The proof's simplistic elegance impressed the reviewers," where "simple" is the intended compliment.

28. **untenable.** Untenable means impossible to defend or maintain, not merely painful. "Her original position became untenable once the counterexample surfaced." Not: "The workload was untenable" when the meaning is that it was exhausting rather than logically indefensible (use "unsustainable" only if that is the intended sense, or "grueling").

29. **affect / effect.** Affect is usually a verb meaning to influence; effect is usually a noun meaning a result, though effect can also work as a verb meaning to bring about. "The dosage did not affect recovery time." "The dosage produced no measurable effect on recovery time." "The committee moved to effect a change in policy" (bring about a change), which differs from "the committee's decision will affect policy" (influence policy).

30. **lie / lay.** Lie means to recline and does not take a direct object (lie, lies, lay, lain); lay means to set something down and does take a direct object (lay, lays, laid, laid). "The samples lie undisturbed in the archive." Not: "The samples lay undisturbed" for the present tense (that is the past tense of lie). "The technician lays the slide on the tray before laying out the reagents."

# Dashes

Dashes are optional punctuation. No grammar rule forces you to use one, and stacking several into one paragraph makes writing feel jumpy. Reach for a dash only when a comma or period would lose something you want to keep.

Don't confuse a dash with a hyphen. A hyphen is the short mark that joins words acting as a single unit ("well-documented", "co-authored") or that appears inside a spelled word ("re-enter"). A dash is longer and stands between larger chunks of a sentence, not between two words fused into one.

## 1. Emphasize material at the start or end of a sentence

A dash before the closing element pulls the reader's eye onto it, the way a drumroll works. Parentheses hide information; a dash spotlights it.

Example: For thirty years she kept the letter unopened, unable to face what her father might have written—until the day of his funeral.

Example: A minor software glitch or the reason three satellites went silent for six hours—which explanation do you believe?

## 2. Emphasize material in the middle of a sentence

A pair of dashes can bracket a phrase, or even a full clause, inside another sentence when you want that inserted material to stand out rather than fade into the background the way parenthetical text would.

Example: The recipe—handwritten, stained with decades of use—had been passed down through four generations of the family.

Example (full clause inside the dashes): The interns—they had only started that Monday—were asked to present findings to the board by Friday.

## 3. Emphasize a descriptive phrase attached to a noun

Dashes can also isolate a modifier that describes a noun, when you want that description read with extra weight rather than treated as incidental detail.

Example: The harbor—choked with fog and eerily silent—gave no sign that a fleet had sailed from it that morning.

Example: The professor—visibly exhausted—still stayed an extra hour to answer questions.

## 4. Mark a list that introduces or wraps up a sentence

A dash can flag a string of items as the setup for what follows, or as a summary of what came before.

Example: Rent, groceries, tuition, a car payment—the graduate's first paycheck disappeared before it arrived.

Example: To cut delivery times, the company overhauled three things—its warehouse layout, its routing software, and its driver scheduling.

## 5. Set off a nonessential phrase that already contains commas

Extra information that isn't required for the sentence to make sense usually gets comma pairs around it. But if that inserted phrase already has commas inside it, adding more commas around it makes the sentence hard to parse. Dashes replace the outer commas so the reader can see where the aside starts and ends.

Confusing with commas: The committee members, tired, underpaid, and thoroughly unimpressed, voted to adjourn early.

Clearer with dashes: The committee members—tired, underpaid, and thoroughly unimpressed—voted to adjourn early.

## 6. Show a break or interruption in spoken dialogue

In quoted speech, a dash marks a speaker trailing off, stumbling, or getting cut off by someone else.

Example: "I never meant to—" she started, before the door slammed shut behind him.

Example: He was halfway through explaining the budget when she cut in: "We already voted on that—move on."

# Dummy subjects

*It* and *there* often open a sentence without pointing to anything. These are dummy subjects: placeholders that fill the subject slot while a weak verb (usually a form of *to be*) does the actual work, and the real subject waits further back in the sentence. Since they add length without adding meaning, cut them wherever a stronger phrasing is available.

## When it isn't a dummy

Not every *it* or *there* at the start of a sentence is empty. Compare:

- Dummy: *It is humid this afternoon.* (*It* refers to nothing. Rewrite as *This afternoon is humid.*)
- Not a dummy: *I bought a used bike, and it is missing a pedal.* (*it* refers back to the bike.)

Only cut the pronoun when nothing in the sentence answers "what does 'it' or 'there' point to?"

## Removing them sharpens the verb

- Wordy: *There are three reviewers who disagree with the proposal.*
- Concise: *Three reviewers disagree with the proposal.*
- Wordy: *It was the storm that knocked out power to the block.*
- Concise: *The storm knocked out power to the block.*

In both pairs, the rewrite is shorter and puts a real actor in front of an active verb (*disagree*, *knocked out*) instead of *are* or *was*. The reader reaches the point faster, and the sentence carries more force.

Dummy subjects aren't a grammar error, and they show up constantly in speech ("It's great to see you," "There's no way that's true"). Academic prose can survive an occasional one. But when a sentence opens with *there is/are/was/were* or an empty *it*, check whether the noun sitting later in the sentence would make a better subject, and move it up.

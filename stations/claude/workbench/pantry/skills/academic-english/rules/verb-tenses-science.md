# Verb tenses in scientific writing

Pick tense by what the sentence describes, not by habit. Default to simple past or simple present; reach for anything fancier only when the meaning actually needs it.

## Past tense for finished work

Use past tense for procedures you ran and observations you made. The experiment is over, so say so.

- *We centrifuged the samples at 4°C for ten minutes before measuring absorbance.*

Use past tense too when reporting what a specific earlier study did or claimed, since that study is a finished event in the historical record.

- *Chen (2011) proposed that mitochondrial density explained the variation.*

A common slip: writers switch that second case into present tense, as if the cited author's paper were speaking to us right now. It isn't. Chen wrote something at a fixed point in the past, and the verb should mark that.

## Present tense for standing facts

Use present tense for claims still held to be true independent of when your study happened. If the finding is general knowledge or a stable relationship, present tense signals that it hasn't expired.

- *Water expands as it freezes.*

This also applies when the finding, not the study that produced it, is the grammatical subject and the citation sits off to the side in parentheses.

- *Larval density strongly predicts adult wing length in this species (Okafor 2015).*

Notice the difference from the past-tense case above: here nobody is doing anything, the fact simply holds, and the parenthetical citation is just pointing to where it was shown.

## Future tense for what comes next

Use future tense sparingly, mainly to flag work you plan to carry out after the current study.

- *In the next phase of this project, we will test whether temperature interacts with the density effect.*

## Rule of thumb

Most of a scientific paper sits in past tense. A smaller share, covering facts that still hold, sits in present tense. Future tense shows up rarely, reserved for stated next steps.

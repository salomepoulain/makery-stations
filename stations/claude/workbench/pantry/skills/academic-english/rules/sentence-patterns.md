# Sentence patterns

Vary clause structure across a paragraph. Prose that repeats one pattern sentence after sentence reads flat even when every sentence is grammatically correct.

## Subject, verb, clause

A sentence needs a subject (who or what) and a verb (the action or state) that together form a complete thought. A clause is any subject-verb pair.

Bare subject-verb pairs:

Reactors overheat.

Signals decay.

Add modifiers and the sentence carries more information but stays a single clause:

Occasionally, reactors overheat without warning.

Weak signals decay faster than strong ones over the same distance.

Two clause types matter for varying structure. An independent clause makes a complete thought and can stand alone as a sentence. A dependent clause has a subject and verb but doesn't make a complete thought on its own, so it has to attach to an independent clause.

## Compound subjects and verbs

A single clause can hold more than one subject or more than one verb without becoming two clauses. It stays one clause as long as every subject in it precedes every verb in it.

Compound subject, one verb:

The technician and her supervisor reviewed the log.

One subject, compound verb:

The technician reviewed the log and flagged three anomalies.

Compound subject with compound verb:

The technician, her supervisor, and the on-call engineer reviewed and revised the incident report twice.

## The four patterns

S stands for subject, V for verb. Arrangement options appear in parentheses; connectors and their punctuation are the part that changes between arrangements.

### Pattern 1: simple sentence

One independent clause. (SV.)

The server crashed.

I noticed.

Look through a draft for sentences built from exactly one clause. If most sentences fall into this pattern, the writing will feel choppy no matter how correct each sentence is.

### Pattern 2: compound sentence

Two or more independent clauses joined as equals. (SV, and SV.) or (SV; however, SV.)

Connectors that take a comma before them: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

Connectors that take a semicolon before and a comma after: however, moreover, nevertheless, nonetheless, therefore.

Examples:

The server crashed twice this week, so we rolled back the deployment.

The rollback fixed the immediate outage; however, it didn't explain the root cause.

Find each of these connectors in your own draft, then identify the subject and verb on both sides of it. If you can't find a full clause on each side, it isn't a compound sentence and the punctuation before the connector needs to change.

### Pattern 3: complex sentence

One independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses. (SV because SV.) or (Because SV, SV.) or (S, because SV, V.)

The connector sits at the start of the dependent clause and signals how that clause relates to the independent one:

Cause/effect: because, since, so that
Comparison/contrast: although, even though, though, whereas, while
Place/manner: where, wherever, how, however
Possibility/condition: if, whether, unless
Relation: that, which, who, whom
Time: after, as, before, since, when, whenever, while, until

Examples:

We rolled back the deployment because the error rate had tripled.

Although the rollback resolved the outage, the underlying bug remained unpatched.

The engineer, because she had seen the pattern before, isolated the faulty node within minutes.

He restarts the service whenever memory usage crosses the threshold.

Though the fix was small, it took three reviewers to approve it.

A dependent clause can sit before, after, or inside the independent clause it belongs to, so when you're scanning for these, check both sides of the connector for a full subject-verb pair.

### Pattern 4: compound-complex sentence

Two or more independent clauses plus one or more dependent clauses, combining the connectors from patterns 2 and 3. (SV, and SV because SV.) or (Because SV, SV, but SV.)

Example:

The team knew the patch was risky, but because the outage was costing revenue every minute, they deployed it anyway, and the error rate dropped within the hour.

## Checking your own balance

Mark each sentence in a paragraph by which pattern it uses. If one pattern dominates, or one never appears, ask:

Could a compound-complex sentence be split into two simpler ones?
Could two short simple sentences next to each other be combined?
Would a different clause order (dependent clause first vs. second) read better here?
Would a different connector, chosen for the relationship it signals, fit better than the one you used?

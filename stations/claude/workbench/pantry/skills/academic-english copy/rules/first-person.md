# First person in academic writing

Whether "I" or "we" belongs in a paper depends on field and purpose, not on a blanket rule against it.

## When first person helps

1. **Ownership of a claim.** Use "I" to mark which argument or contribution is yours, rather than something already established in the field. "I argue that the 1990 reform changed enforcement patterns more than it changed the underlying law" is sharper than "It could be argued that the reform changed enforcement patterns."
2. **Avoiding contorted sentences.** Dropping first person to sidestep "I" often produces passive or roundabout phrasing. "We tested three cache eviction policies under load" reads better than "Three cache eviction policies were tested under load by the researchers," which hides who did the testing behind a vague noun.
3. **Locating your work relative to others.** First person marks the seam between what prior work established and what you add. "Chen et al. modeled latency under fixed bandwidth; I extend their model to variable bandwidth" is clearer than trying to describe that extension without naming an actor.

## When first person is a problem

1. **Personal detail that doesn't serve the argument.** "I first got curious about coral bleaching after a diving trip in 2019" belongs in a memoir, not in the introduction to a paper on bleaching thresholds. Cut personal narrative that doesn't change what the reader needs to know.
2. **Opinion standing in for evidence.** "I feel that remote juries are less reliable" is weaker than a claim backed by a study. If a sentence would only be true because you said it, and nothing else supports it, either find the support or mark it clearly as speculation.
3. **Overuse that centers the writer instead of the subject.** A paragraph where every sentence opens with "I" reads as a diary, not an analysis. "I looked at the data. I noticed a pattern. I think it matters." shifts attention from the pattern to the writer's process of noticing it.

## General usage guidance

- First person sits most naturally in introductions (stating what the paper does), statements of your central claim, conclusions, and methods sections (describing what you did).
- Skip "I believe that" and "I think that" as a lead-in to a claim. Anything you assert without a citation is already understood as your position, so the phrase adds words without adding meaning: "Enforcement weakened after 1990" already carries your authorship.
- In a paper with multiple authors, "we" refers strictly to that paper's authors carrying out the work. It is not a stand-in for "readers in general" or "people in this field."

## By discipline

- **Sciences.** Historically written around first person entirely, using passive constructions like "the sample was heated to 80°C." That convention is loosening, and "I" or "we" now shows up more often when describing methodology or explaining how a project's approach differs from prior ones.
- **Social sciences.** Follows a pattern close to the sciences, with more room for "I" when the author is describing their own study design, fieldwork, or interpretive stance.
- **Humanities.** First person is common and often expected, since these fields tend to value showing who is making a claim and on what grounds, rather than obscuring the claimant behind passive phrasing.
- **Technical writing.** First person is rare. Documentation and technical reports usually keep the focus on the system or process being described rather than on the author describing it.
